01 / Introduction · AI-Enabled SDLC Field Guide

What changes when AI writes, reviews, approves, or deploys software?

AI is becoming part of how software is developed and delivered. It can produce code, recommend decisions, use engineering tools, authorize changes within delegated policies, and participate in production operations.

Those capabilities can improve delivery. They also change how failures occur, who has authority, which safeguards matter, and what evidence demonstrates that a system is operating as intended.

What this Field Guide is for

A practical, scenario-based resource for Engineering, Security, GRC, and Internal Audit to understand AI-enabled SDLC risks, design stronger delivery systems, and evaluate whether the resulting decisions and outcomes can be trusted.

Learn without an API token or internal company data. All examples in this prototype are fictional.
The difference AI makes

Four shifts that change software delivery risk

Many underlying SDLC risks already existed. AI changes the authority, reach, variability, and evidence surrounding them.

01

Assistance → action

AI can move from suggesting code or reviews to running tools, submitting approvals, and initiating workflows. Who decides and who executes may differ.

02

One change → wider exposure

A small change to shared code can affect customers, security, billing, or other dependent systems. A short diff is not necessarily a low-impact change.

03

Stable rules → changing behavior

Models, prompts, context, tool access, and external providers can change behavior without an obvious source-code change.

04

Recorded activity → proven authority

A log can show an approval occurred without proving who made the decision, what was reviewed, or whether the action was authorized.

WHY THIS GUIDE EXISTS

From governance principles to engineering decisions

NIST AI RMF, NIST SSDF and related guidance offer useful foundations for managing AI and software-delivery risk. The challenge is applying those principles to a specific workflow: what AI may decide or execute, what limits its authority, and what evidence supports reliance.

Where does AI participate—and what authority does it have?

Two dimensions, not a risk score

03 / Independent assurance

What evidence would justify reliance on AI-enabled software delivery?

Start with a specific management claim. Independently establish what was implemented, challenge the effective boundary, verify evidence completeness and draw a conclusion no broader than the results support.

WHY ASSURANCE MUST EVOLVE

A recorded approval is not the same as a demonstrated decision.

Even before AI, audits of software delivery had hard questions: Was the code review meaningful? Did the effective CI/CD controls prevent bypass? Were the deployment populations complete? Could an unauthorized change reach production through another route?

AI increases the importance of these questions by participating in code creation, review, authorization, and execution. Traditional walkthroughs, sampled approvals, and point-in-time tests remain useful—but examining whether a recorded step occurred may not establish whether the end-to-end delivery system actually protected the change. Better delivery systems can produce version-bound decision records, traceable releases and independently reconcilable event populations from the beginning.

MANAGEMENT ASSERTION

“Only approved low-risk changes can receive automated authorization, and policy changes are independently governed.”

INDEPENDENT ASSURANCE OBJECTIVE

AI decisions and actions remain within a documented, appropriately approved business and technical boundary.

PRACTICAL TEST / CHALLENGE
  1. Trace an actual use case from business objective to owner, authorized actions, prohibited actions and approved scope.
  2. Compare the approved policy and decision rights with effective repository rulesets, service credentials and administration permissions.
  3. Attempt an excluded or unauthorized change, and review who can change eligibility or bypass restrictions.
EVIDENCE TO REQUEST

Approved operating scope, risk treatment record, versioned policy and change history, actual permissions, ruleset/bypass configuration, exception authorizations and test results.

POPULATION & RELIABILITY CHECK

Determine the full set of agents, repositories and eligible routes; reconcile ownership and policy versions to configuration independently obtained from the platform.

WHAT WOULD CONTRADICT THE CLAIM?

An unauthorized actor can alter the rule or approve an excluded change, or management cannot establish who owns the decision.

LIMIT OF RELIANCE

A governance approval alone supports intended authority, not proof of independent technical enforcement or sustained operation.

Population, sampling and source reliability
Determine the full population and sources first; test completeness and accuracy of system-generated reports and reconcile where practical. Select sample coverage based on risk, frequency, scope, relevant methodology and intended reliance—not a universal 25-sample rule. A single negative test may support a design conclusion but not sustained operating effectiveness.
Nondeterministic AI evaluations and reviewer quality
Use independently adjudicated representative, high-severity and adversarial scenarios; track false-low-risk outcomes, correlation between reviewers, prompt/model versions and changes in runtime behavior. Model agreement is neither independent authorization nor proof of classification reliability.
Agent lifecycle and trust boundaries
Consider software supply-chain provenance, agent identity and tool permissions, untrusted input and output boundaries, persistent context, runtime monitoring, behavior changes and decommissioning. To assess effective restrictions, examine configuration, access paths, negative tests and recorded tool activity independently of agent instructions. This is general assurance guidance, not evidence of an implemented control.
Frameworks and external assurance claims
NIST SSDF, NIST AI RMF, OWASP and ISO/IEC 42001 can inform objectives and test questions. SOX and SOC 2 relevance depends on the system, criteria and reporting scope. A conceptual crosswalk does not establish that an individual framework mandates a specific GitHub setting, WORM retention period or sample size.
