ASSURANCE / AN OPEN DESIGN CHALLENGE

Two AI reviewers agree.
Is that enough to trust the approval?

Not necessarily. AI can develop, review, approve or execute changes. Each role creates different questions about authority, independence and evidence. This field guide uses a human-developed, AI-reviewed approval path to explore how Engineering, Risk and Audit might gain confidence in the whole system—not just its model outputs.

Illustrative, not prescriptive. This is a proposed evaluation approach for discussion, not claims of implemented controls, verified vendor assurances, SOX compliance or audit reliance.

01 / THE PROPOSED OPERATING MODEL

Know what AI does—and who controls it.

A reviewer is not a developer; a sign-off is not a deployment. In this illustration, humans write code and merge PRs. AI contributes review and a qualifying sign-off; non-AI controls enforce the rest.

  1. Human developerCreates PR
  2. Deterministic eligibilityCompany-governed scope and exclusions
  3. Two AI reviewersVendor-managed review service + company-owned review logic on cloud-hosted infrastructure. Matching qualifying results are required, but agreement is not correctness.
  4. AI sign-off and human mergeReview authorization and merge execution are distinct
  5. Non-AI release controlsPost-merge checks, artifact promotion, deployment and PR-to-production reconciliation

Shared responsibility is part of the test. Identify who can change the model, runtime, prompts, reviewer code, policies, permissions and input data. Review the vendor's applicable SOC report, exceptions and customer responsibilities; neither provider assurance nor company code ownership alone validates the integration.

02 / BEFORE RELYING ON AI

Start with what can override the AI.

If a bot can change its own approval rules, an administrator can bypass protection, or a dependency map is wrong, two matching review outcomes may provide little comfort.

Enforcement and bypass

Global branch protection, CODEOWNERS, required checks, emergency routes and direct-to-production access.

Authority and separation

Independent ownership of approval policy, bot logic and deployment paths; service-account privileges and access reviews.

Vendor and agent security

Shared responsibility, SOC report scope, tool permissions, prompt injection and restrictions on bot self-modification.

Trusted inputs

Repository classification, labels, tagging and dependency mappings: provenance, completeness and who can alter them.

03 / BEFORE ENABLEMENT

Don't go live on two matching scores.

Baseline configurations, security review, QA, false-data injection, independently adjudicated AI results, parallel testing, human sampling and management sign-off belong in the implementation plan. Test correlated false-low-risk misses and verify that uncertainty routes to humans.

Assurance objectiveEvidence needed to assess it
Change eligibilityValidate repository exclusions, ownership and downstream dependency maps. Inject a hidden billing dependency and a missing map; verify mandatory human routing.
AI reliabilityIndependently adjudicate real and adversarial PR cases; measure consequential false-low-risk classifications and test two-model correlated misses.
AuthorizationInspect effective GitHub rulesets, bot privileges and CODEOWNER routes; test absent/skipped/stale AI outcomes and privileged bypass attempts.
Policy integrityTrace approved model, prompt, mapping and rule changes; attempt self-relaxing policy edits; verify current revision and policy version binding.
Deployment integrityReperform failed/skipped CI gates, source-to-artifact checks, and approval-to-production version/digest tracing.
Continuous monitoringReconcile complete independent merge/deploy populations to recorded approvals; test missing events, alerts, escalation and suspension.

Test design should cover prompt injection, least privilege, secret handling and enforcement independence alongside these objectives—not as a disconnected security checklist.

04 / WHILE THE SYSTEM OPERATES

Continuously watch the controls, not just the bots.

Configuration drift: Changes to rulesets, CODEOWNERS, model or prompt versions, permissions, labels and dependency data must be identified and governed.

Behavioral checks: Repeated negative tests, reviewer disagreement, skipped evaluations and false-low-risk misses need expected outcomes and escalation.

Production traceability: Reconcile deployed code to deployment job, Artifactory artifact, source SHA and PR; tie incidents back to decisions and govern subsequent fixes.

Alert health: Monitor data freshness, missing events, alert delivery, exception ownership and the ability to suspend autonomous approval.

Ownership: First line operates and remediates; second line challenges thresholds and exceptions; third line independently evaluates control evidence. An AI assurance challenger that continuously checks expected versus observed controls is a future hypothesis; its independence, feasibility and cost remain open for discussion.

05 / THE ASSURANCE CONCLUSION

What evidence would actually justify reliance?

Continuous monitoring makes evidence available; it does not independently verify that evidence. An auditor still needs complete source populations, effective historical configurations, reperformance, exception resolution and period-appropriate testing. Point-in-time testing cannot establish operation over an entire period by itself.

Design

Are authority, scope, control owners and failure responses appropriate?

Implementation

Are protections and negative tests actually configured and effective?

Operation over time

Do complete records support decisions, interventions and approved changes during the period?

Compliance evaluation addresses applicable control and reporting requirements; broader risk management also considers model reliability, vendor exposure, security and operational resilience. The sufficiency of each requires its own judgment.
