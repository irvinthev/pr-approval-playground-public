Engineering accountability in the AI-enabled SDLC

AI can do more of the work. Engineering still owns the outcome.

There's a lot of excitement about autonomous coding, review and release. Fair enough. But I think we're asking the wrong first question when we ask whether AI is allowed to do something.

The better question is: what engineering problem are we solving, what decisions are we delegating, and how would we know the new workflow is actually better?

Author's research journal · Working account

How I ended up asking these questions

This started with a practical software-delivery problem: how do you reduce the wait for pull-request review without making the quality of the decision—or the path into production—worse?

My background is in technology risk and audit, so the familiar starting point would have been to examine approval requirements and whether the change-management controls were still satisfied. That's a fair constraint. But it didn't answer the more interesting engineering question: what problem does the approval process actually solve, and which parts of it are worth keeping?

I started reading beyond approval controls. In DevOps, continuous integration and continuous delivery, the point is not to make people click through more gates. It's to move smaller changes through reliable automated feedback, make defects visible earlier and shorten the path from observation to improvement. That made me reconsider how we treat review, testing, deployment and monitoring as separate activities.

Then AI changed the question again. A coding assistant might only suggest a change. A tool-using agent might create it, validate it, request a review or act on a repository through credentials. A model's “low risk” conclusion might merely inform a person—or be converted into a decision that allows a merge. Those aren't interchangeable designs.

01

I questioned the existing workflow

Why does a PR wait? Is the delay valuable technical review, reviewer routing, expensive CI, or simply handoffs? Would a narrower human fast-track be a better intervention than autonomous approval?

02

I followed the actual execution path

What does GitHub enforce through CODEOWNERS, rulesets, required checks, review dismissal and bypass actors? What changes when an agent uses GitHub tools, and can the approval be tied to the exact reviewed revision?

03

I investigated how the agents work

I examined questions around Claude Code, Cursor, Bugbot and GitHub-connected agents: what context do they consume, what can they execute, which permissions matter, and how do model outputs become workflow actions?

04

I returned to evidence and outcomes

Could I reconstruct the original instruction, agent actions, authorization decision, merged code and deployed artifact? Would I notice a failure? More importantly, would the new workflow improve engineering quality and throughput after rework and operating cost?

What I researched—and what each source helped me question

I didn't find one framework that answered the whole problem. Different sources illuminated different pieces of the engineering system.

DevOps and secure delivery

NIST SSDF and NIST's DevSecOps reference model helped frame repeatable secure development, automated feedback and evidence from build through operation.

AI behavior and trust boundaries

NIST AI RMF, OWASP GenAI, CoSAI and Google SAIF informed questions about variable model behavior, untrusted inputs, evaluation and agentic tool access.

Engineering platform reality

GitHub rulesets, CODEOWNERS, and official Claude Code and Cursor documentation directed attention to actual execution permissions, configuration and product-dependent behavior.

Assurance and accountability

COSO and traditional assurance principles helped distinguish management's claims from independently supportable conclusions. They do not replace investigation of how the engineering platform actually behaves.

Where continuous monitoring changed my thinking

I've become less interested in whether a single approval record exists and more interested in whether the engineering system can explain the whole change. Observability lets us reconstruct what happened. Monitoring detects conditions worth investigating. Continuous assurance requires trustworthy, sufficiently complete evidence and a defined response—not just another dashboard or alert stream.

The questions I kept coming back to

  1. What problem are we actually solving, and is AI the best way to solve it?
  2. What is the difference between an agent recommending, executing and authorizing?
  3. What enforces the boundary when the agent misinterprets instructions or the model is wrong?
  4. Are we trusting familiar human approval processes because they work—or because they're familiar?
  5. Can we see and reconstruct the path from intent to deployed outcome?
  6. What evidence would make me change my conclusion?

This is a reflective synthesis of the research questions behind the Field Guide, not a date-by-date research log or a claim that every source directly endorses the resulting method. The examples are public and illustrative; no internal implementation is described here.

Part I · Understand the engineering shift

What changes when agents join software delivery?

An assistant that suggests a function and an agent that can merge or deploy that function are not two versions of the same risk. They're different engineering architectures.

By AI-enabled SDLC, I mean the software development lifecycle in which AI models or agents assist with or perform design, coding, testing, review, release, and operations. This is about using AI to build and operate software—not necessarily about building AI products or establishing a new governance office.

Engineering has delegated work for decades. CI systems run tests. Bots create pull requests. Deployment pipelines move artifacts into production. Agents extend that pattern because they can interpret an objective, consume contextual material, select intermediate steps, and invoke tools rather than simply execute a fixed sequence.

That flexibility is the opportunity. It's also why the label “AI assistant” tells us surprisingly little about what the system can affect.

Five kinds of participation—not one AI category

Assist

Suggest code, tests, designs or explanations for a person to evaluate.

Execute

Change files, run commands, invoke APIs or open a pull request using assigned permissions.

Recommend

Evaluate a change or produce a risk judgment that another actor may rely upon.

Authorize

Approve a merge, accept an exception or permit a release within a delegated decision boundary.

Operate

Diagnose, change or recover systems over time, potentially without a fresh human instruction for every action.

These activities overlap. A model can influence a human decision without holding approval permission, while an agent can hold broad tool access even when a human formally owns the final decision.

The real shift is between intent and effect

Imagine a coding agent assigned a narrow defect fix. It reads a ticket, repository instructions, source comments and tool results. Some are reliable context; others may be outdated, mistaken or deliberately hostile. The agent can also have permissions that reach beyond the code it was asked to change.

Existing problems—excess privilege, inadequate tests, unsafe releases—haven't disappeared or suddenly become “AI risks.” But an agent changes how those weaknesses might be triggered, chained together, or detected. In some environments the existing branch protections, identities and CI checks already do the job. In others, they protect a narrower path than the agent can actually take.

Useful distinction

Unpredictable model judgment does not require unpredictable authorization. The design can allow a model to recommend an action while restricting what it can execute through independently enforced policy and permissions.

Leave with a question worth investigating

Where is the actual authority in this workflow?

Don't start with the model name or a diagram of agents talking to other agents. Identify the identities, tools, data, reachable systems and decisions that can change real engineering state. What can happen without another person intervening?

Part II · Make engineering choices

What should Engineering design—and what should it stop doing?

I wouldn't start by automating today's approval ritual. I'd start by asking why the review takes so long and whether the review actually improves the change.

Slow pull requests can have different causes: reviewer availability, too many mandatory approvers, unclear ownership, expensive CI, poor change packaging, or a real need for domain expertise. AI auto-approval addresses only some of those problems. If the bottleneck is an unnecessary review route, improving routing may deliver most of the benefit without delegating approval authority to a model.

There is a credible competing argument here: existing engineering safeguards may already constrain a narrowly scoped assistant. Adding another policy engine, evaluation program or monitoring layer could make the system more expensive and harder to operate without improving reliability. We should take that argument seriously, especially for low-authority assistance.

Conversely, an agent with GitHub write permissions, access to privileged tools or the ability to change its own instructions is not equivalent to an autocomplete feature. The architecture should follow the capabilities and consequences, not a blanket rule about AI.

Worked engineering decision · Fictional scenario

A team wants to reduce PR review latency. Which architecture earns its complexity?

The engineering objective is to reduce time waiting for review while preserving the benefits of meaningful change validation. Consider three implementable designs before deciding that autonomous approval is the answer.

Hypothetical alternatives, not actual performance results or prescribed solutions. Their value depends on the implementation, engineering constraints and measured outcomes.

The uncomfortable questionWhat if the easiest 80% of the latency improvement comes from removing unnecessary routing—not from granting an agent authority to approve code?

Better engineering also means giving AI more useful work

We shouldn't evaluate these tools only by whether they avoid bad decisions. AI can help teams generate tests, examine edge cases, navigate unfamiliar dependencies, analyze logs, and identify suspicious patterns. Those activities might strengthen the work humans and CI systems already do.

But code generated quickly can create more review burden. Tests written by the same agent that wrote the code may repeat the same mistaken assumptions. More comments do not necessarily mean better review. The relevant question is whether the whole workflow improves in quality, speed and reliability.

Quality

More useful validation

Assess defect detection, severity of misses, test independence and production escapes—not model agreement alone.

Productivity

Less total work

Count review, rework, evaluation maintenance and recovery—not merely generation speed or time to first comment.

Reliability

Better recovery

Validate rollback, stoppability, human fallback, and reconstruction of consequential actions.

Authority → Enforcement → Evidence is a design question, not a checklist

AuthorityWho decides? What did the human actually authorize? What scope has been delegated?
EnforcementWhat independent restriction prevents the agent from exceeding that scope?
EvidenceWhat can we reconstruct from trusted platform events, revisions and outcomes?

Reuse repository rulesets, CI gates, identity systems and deployment restrictions where they work. Add new safeguards only where the agent-enabled path exposes a material gap.

Leave with a question worth investigating

Which design improves the engineering outcome with the least unnecessary complexity?

Compare the AI-enabled proposal against the best realistic non-autonomous alternative. If a simpler routing change performs as well, say so. If autonomy wins, show the conditions that make it safe enough and worth operating.

Part III · Substantiate the claims

How would we know the workflow actually works?

A green pipeline, a passing evaluation and a GitHub approval are useful observations. None, by itself, establishes that the right change was authorized, safely delivered, or worth the effort.

Start with the decision the evidence is supposed to support. Are we deciding whether an agent may write code, call a GitHub API, approve a change, or take an operational action? The depth of evaluation should match the consequence of being wrong.

Where the agent can take consequential actions, examine the actual workflow: tool permissions, untrusted context, approval boundaries, code revisions, merge and deployment routes, and recovery. A model benchmark with no access to the real tools or repository can answer a useful but narrower question about model behavior.

Then ask whether the records are complete and independent enough to reconstruct what happened. An agent's own explanation is not an adequate substitute for trusted GitHub, CI and deployment events. Equally, retaining everything the agent sees may introduce confidentiality and data-retention problems. Evidence needs engineering judgment too.

Three questions that should change an assurance conclusion

Claim

What was supposed to happen?

Identify intended task scope, effective authorization and the engineer's or management's actual decision.

Technical challenge

What happens when something goes wrong?

Try a stale approval, an excluded change, a misleading classification, a tool bypass or a lost event.

Reliable evidence

Can we prove the result?

Correlate exact revisions, independent platform restrictions, agent actions and production outcomes. State what remains unobserved.

Judge the economics of the whole workflow

Faster does not automatically mean better. Compare elapsed cycle time, reviewer attention, defect escape, security findings, incident recovery, tool cost and evaluation maintenance. A system that saves an hour waiting for approval but creates two hours of rework hasn't delivered the promised benefit.

The research informing the thesis reports different productivity outcomes in different settings. That is an argument for local, workflow-specific measurement—not for assuming AI always helps, and not for assuming it always slows engineers down. Evidence from narrow test-generation implementations likewise supports potential, not universal effectiveness.

This is also where existing safeguards deserve a fair challenge. A mandatory human review may be recorded without being meaningful. An AI evaluation may be reproducible without being relevant. Neither should be treated as effective simply because it produces an artifact.

Research foundation

Methodology, sources and the limits of this guide

These examples are an independent engineering synthesis, informed by the working thesis Engineering Accountability in the AI-Enabled SDLC (Round 4 drafts), the Field Guide's fictional 21-scenario catalog, and public technical guidance. They are not claimed as the final approved thesis text, a company's implemented controls, or a certified framework.

Reference families include NIST SSDF, NIST AI RMF, OWASP GenAI Security, CoSAI, Google SAIF, COSO, and vendor documentation for GitHub, Anthropic, Cursor and OpenAI.

What this material can and cannot establish

Standards describe principles and practices; vendors document features; research can demonstrate results within specific studied settings. None independently verifies the configuration, operating effectiveness or economics of a different engineering workflow. The architecture comparison above is fictional; no source is presented as evidence that one option wins universally.

Publication of the separate research crosswalk remains contingent on validation of provision-level mappings and a public-content review.
